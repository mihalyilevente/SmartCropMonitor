# Urea (CO(NH₂)₂)

## Overview

**Urea** is the most widely used **nitrogen (N) fertilizer** in global agriculture. It is a synthetic organic compound containing **46% nitrogen**, making it the most concentrated solid nitrogen fertilizer available.

Due to its high nitrogen content, low cost per unit N, and transport efficiency, urea is a primary nitrogen source in intensive cereal, maize, rice, and horticultural systems.

However, urea is also highly sensitive to management. Misapplication leads to significant **nitrogen losses via volatilization, leaching, and crop damage risk** if improperly handled. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

# Chemical Properties

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Chemical formula | CO(NH₂)₂ |
| Nitrogen content | 46% |
| Physical form | Granules / prills |
| Solubility | Highly water soluble |
| pH effect (initial) | Temporarily alkaline zone in soil |

After application, urea is rapidly hydrolyzed by the enzyme **urease**, converting it into ammonium forms usable by plants.

# Transformation in Soil

Urea does not act directly as plant-available nitrogen. It undergoes several transformations:

## 1. Hydrolysis
Urea → ammonium carbonate (via urease activity)

- Occurs within hours to days
- Strongly influenced by temperature and moisture

## 2. Ammonium formation
- NH₄⁺ becomes available for plant uptake
- Can be retained on soil colloids (CEC binding)

## 3. Nitrification
- NH₄⁺ → NO₃⁻
- Driven by soil bacteria
- Increases leaching risk

---

# Nitrogen Loss Pathways

![Urea pathway](./images/img.png)

## 1. Ammonia volatilization (major loss pathway)

When urea is surface-applied, a high-pH zone forms temporarily, leading to **NH₃ gas losses to the atmosphere**.

- Losses can exceed 30% under unfavorable conditions
- In extreme cases, up to ~60% nitrogen loss reported

### Risk factors:
- High temperature
- Dry soil surface
- High pH soils
- Surface residues (no-till systems)
- Wind exposure

---

## 2. Leaching

After nitrification:
- Nitrate (NO₃⁻) is highly mobile
- Moves below root zone in sandy soils or high rainfall systems

---

## 3. Denitrification

Occurs in:
- Waterlogged soils
- Anaerobic conditions

NO₃⁻ → N₂ / N₂O (gas losses, greenhouse gas emissions)

# Agronomic Benefits of Urea

## Advantages

- High nitrogen concentration (46%)
- Low transport cost per unit N
- Compatible with multiple application methods
- Suitable for dry and irrigated systems
- Can be blended with other fertilizers

# Crop Response

Urea is widely used in:

- Wheat and barley (top-dressing)
- Maize (split applications)
- Rice (flooded systems with adaptations)
- Pastures and forage systems
- Vegetables and horticultural crops

Nitrogen from urea is essential for:
- Chlorophyll synthesis
- Vegetative growth
- Protein formation
- Yield formation in cereals

---

# Application Methods

## 1. Soil incorporation (preferred)

- Incorporate immediately after application
- Minimizes volatilization losses
- Most efficient method

---

## 2. Surface application (risky)

If surface-applied:
- Apply before rainfall or irrigation
- Use urease inhibitors where appropriate
- Avoid hot, dry conditions

---

## 3. Band placement

- Place urea 5–7 cm below surface or away from seed
- Reduces seed burn risk
- Improves nitrogen use efficiency

---

## 4. Foliar application (limited use)

- Low concentrations only
- Risk of leaf burn at high rates
- Used mainly for correction rather than bulk N supply

---

# Environmental Conditions Affecting Efficiency

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| High temperature | Increases volatilization |
| Low soil moisture | Slows incorporation, increases loss |
| High pH soil | Increases NH₃ formation |
| High residue cover | Increases urease activity |
| Wind | Increases gas loss |

---

# Best Management Practices (BMPs)

## 1. Timing

- Apply before rainfall or irrigation events
- Avoid application during heat peaks

---

## 2. Incorporation

- Mechanical incorporation or irrigation (>10–15 mm water)

---

## 3. Split application

- Reduces loss risk
- Matches crop nitrogen demand curve

---

## 4. Stabilized fertilizers

- Urease inhibitors (e.g., NBPT)
- Nitrification inhibitors (where appropriate)

---

## 5. Soil testing

- Base nitrogen decisions on soil analysis
- Avoid over-application

---

# Fertilizer Compatibility

## Compatible with:
- DAP (diammonium phosphate)
- MAP (monoammonium phosphate)
- Potassium chloride (KCl)

## Caution:
- Avoid mixing with materials prone to moisture reaction
- Avoid improper storage in high humidity

# Economic Importance

Urea dominates global nitrogen fertilizer markets due to:

- Lowest cost per kg N
- Efficient logistics
- High availability
- Industrial production scalability

However, economic efficiency depends strongly on:
- Application method
- Weather conditions
- Soil type

---

# Environmental Considerations

## Greenhouse gas emissions

- Indirect N₂O emissions from nitrification/denitrification
- NH₃ volatilization contributes to nitrogen deposition elsewhere

## Water pollution risk

- Nitrate leaching into groundwater systems
- Eutrophication of surface waters

---

# Precision Agriculture Applications

Modern nitrogen management increasingly uses:

- NDVI-based nitrogen status mapping
- Variable rate fertilizer application
- Soil electrical conductivity mapping
- Weather-based nitrogen modeling
- Crop growth stage algorithms

These tools significantly improve nitrogen use efficiency and reduce losses.

---

# Common Management Errors

- Surface application without incorporation
- Applying before dry, hot periods
- Excess nitrogen application beyond crop demand
- Ignoring soil pH and residue conditions
- No use of inhibitors in high-risk conditions

---

# Key Takeaways

- Urea is the most widely used nitrogen fertilizer globally due to its high nitrogen concentration and low cost.
- Its efficiency depends strongly on **rapid incorporation into soil or rainfall after application**.
- The main agronomic risk is **ammonia volatilization from surface application**.
- Proper management (timing, placement, inhibitors) can significantly increase nitrogen use efficiency.
- Urea should be treated as a high-performance but high-sensitivity input requiring agronomic control.
